Noisy Times 3: The Decline of the Will to Know

By Lesandro Ponciano, on September 9, 2026.

What happens to a society when people begin treating their decisions as a gamble or a game of trial and error? On what is conduct anchored in a world that disdains knowledge? In the Analects, concerning the teachings of Confucius, we learn that there are three methods by which we may learn wisdom. The first, and noblest, is by reflection, which consists of thinking about things and using reason. The second, and easiest, is by observation, which consists of noticing and copying the example of others. The third, and bitterest, is by experience, which happens through living out one's own mistakes and successes. Those who are capable of reflection and observation benefit from the experiences and conclusions of those who lived through a situation before, seeking decisions with predictable outcomes. Meanwhile, for those incapable of reflection and observation, all that remains is personal experience based on gambling and trial-and-error. Consequently, they often repeat mistakes that others have already committed.

When society reflects and observes less, the gambling culture grows. Between 2020 and 2023, I was away from in-person undergraduate classrooms due to the pandemic and a post-doctoral research fellowship. Upon returning to teaching, I noticed a new phenomenon. Behaviors emerged that should have been exotic, but had become surprisingly common. It became routine to see some university students walk into class without a backpack, pencil, pen, book, or notebook. They were individuals holding nothing but their smartphones, sitting down and repeating this pattern day after day. These students genuinely believed they did not need to take notes or reflect on the material. They gambled that simply having heard about the topic would be enough to learn and answer exam questions correctly. Following the "path of least resistance," their mindset was: "what if he only asks trivial questions?". Naturally, this approach did not work in courses like the ones I taught, which were rooted in logic, calculations, algorithm execution, and data structures.

I used to warn them: "this algorithm has several subtle details; don't leave running it for the first time on the exam; practice beforehand." However, the warning was received more as an empty threat than as guidance from someone familiar with the topic's complexity. A logic took root: "I'll try doing it without studying and see what happens." When they realized the subject's complexity, they ran into two problems: they no longer knew how to study that kind of content and had already missed the foundational concepts. This made restarting from scratch far too burdensome, resulting in extremely high failure rates. There is no need to gamble when one can reflect and observe how others solve the same problems. If there is a clear recommendation on how to study a given subject, why ignore it? Today, this superficiality, instant gratification, and difficulty in thinking beyond fragments—distinctive features of online systems—are part of the phenomenon discussed in the essay The Fragmentation of Life.

The gambling mindset of these students and the indifference connected to it were evident on the day of the in-person exam itself. On those occasions, it was common for nearly a dozen students not to bring even a pen or pencil. The gamble, in this case, was that someone nearby would have spare supplies to lend. The start of the exam turned chaotic, with several students walking around the classroom in search of basic writing tools. The situation became so recurring and disruptive that I began sending emails before exams to remind them that bringing their own materials is an individual responsibility. In my childhood at a rural school in countryside Brazil, I remember a teacher saying to a forgetful classmate: "Have you ever seen anyone go to hoe a field without bringing their hoe?" The lesson remains necessary, with the difference that today it must be taught to young adults over 20 years old, whereas it used to be learned in childhood.

If this behavior was already visible in face-to-face teaching, the scenario in synchronous classes and remote exams was even more pronounced. Students gambled that during the exam they would find a way to discover the correct answer, whether by consulting peers or turning to generative artificial intelligence (AI). When I designed assessments with individualized questions immune to direct AI plagiarism, the failure rate was massive. Imagine a professional who lacks mastery of their field's core techniques and gambles that they will figure things out only when a real problem arises in their professional practice. In such cases, society loses trust that those holding each position possess the proper preparation to fulfill it.

Far from being merely a university symptom, this is a broad social phenomenon. Recently, I listened to a podcast in which a high school teacher explained why he had resigned. He told the story of a underprivileged student who did not study, caused constant disruptions in class, and undermined teaching efforts. When questioned seriously about how he envisioned his life five years down the line, the youth replied: "I'll be married, well-employed, owning my house, driving my car, and raising my children." There was a gulf between the desired future and present actions. The envisioned future was merely a gamble, as the student was not creating the conditions to make it viable. The understanding that goals must be pursued step by step to be achieved has been lost. There is a clear trend toward replacing action grounded in knowledge, planning, and strategy with exceptionalism or "magic," in the hope of attaining results without going through the necessary process.

This illusion of a magical shortcut is not entirely new, but it has taken on unprecedented dimensions. In the late 1980s and early 1990s, many girls wanted to be models and boys wanted to be professional soccer players. This was not a healthy childhood fantasy, but a life outlook sustained until age 17 or 18, when it was revealed to be an illusion. Gambling is seductive because it dispenses with plans, evidence, and strategy. It functions like magic, sustained by the idea of "innate talent" or exceptionalism. Today, the equivalent appears in children declaring they will be "influencers," "TikTokers," or "YouTubers." Once again, a gamble. This aspiration would be harmless if it were one option among many, rather than the only one. The pursuit has shifted toward an exceptional path that promises an immediate, extraordinary result, at the expense of a path that yields something more permanent but is long and demands effort, dedication, and consistency. It seems more appealing to gamble everything on replicating short video trends on social media than to develop a profession requiring years of study, as discussed in the essay The Noise of Trends. In an age of instant gratification, many prefer an all-or-nothing solution, betting everything on the short term and running the risk of ending up with nothing in the long run.

In this cultural landscape, the explosion of online sports betting platforms (popularly known in Brazil as bets) comes as no surprise. Treating life as a game of chance was already part of the collective mindset; platforms simply monetized this outlook. The scale of the problem is visible in the billions of dollars moved and the vast numbers of indebted individuals and addicts. Although initial triggers range from entertainment to curiosity, the core issue is the search for a magical exit strategy to reach a life of abundance. In a society marked by extreme social inequality, where upward mobility through traditional work feels slow and distant, gambling emerges as the sole illusory bridge to the extraordinary life displayed across social feeds.

It is important to consider that much of this gambling and trial-and-error behavior relates to confronting the unknown. When we face an entirely new reality, there are insufficient elements for prior observation or reflection, leaving only empirical experience and learning. We live in a era exhibiting these characteristics due to advancements in AI, climate crisis, and public health threats. The chaotic context observed today can lead some people toward reasoning based more on trial and error; however, the trap lies in extending this approach to all life situations. Human Error Theory classifies errors into ignorance, slips, or memory lapses. Gambling as trial and error is characteristic of collective ignorance, when reflective or observational knowledge about a context does not yet exist. In such cases, successive testing to generate learning is the solution. However, when ignorance is merely individual, gambling ceases to be innovation and becomes incompetence, lack of observation, and an absence of reflection.

In scientific research, gambling and trial-and-error have a legitimate role within an exploratory approach, but misuse exists. Bets and trial runs allow researchers to gain initial insights into a poorly understood phenomenon. The problem with this posture arises when investigation devolves into a blind search. The era of massive datasets collected and analyzed by computational systems has intensified this deviation: instead of formulating prior hypotheses and structured questions, an exhaustive data search is conducted, diving blindly into a database in the hope that something interesting will reveal itself by chance. If searching for a needle in a haystack is already difficult, sifting through the entire haystack without knowing what one is looking for becomes pure gambling. Research turns into an agonizing process. Artificial intelligence agents will likely accelerate this practice by automating such sweeps. This may generate anomalies that make science noisy, validating purely circumstantial patterns and correlations stemming from sheer mathematical coincidence, without any causal link or practical value. Known in scientific jargon as p-hacking or data dredging, this harmful approach degrades methodological rigor and adds little to true knowledge.

Part of the chaos of these noisy times stems from an unwillingness to know and individual incompetence that leads to repeating mistakes in situations where extensive knowledge is already readily available. Another part can be attributed to society being induced into random assumptions based on mere circumstantial anomalies arising from gambles or blind searches. In these times, actions become imprecise, defy logic, and result in a chaos born from the inability to comprehend the meaning of each choice. Delegating to machines tasks previously performed solely by human beings should not mean thinking less. Quite the contrary. It becomes necessary to comprehend more complex processes executed at far greater speeds. Otherwise, the world will become a "magical" place in which we fail to understand not only natural phenomena, but also the artificial ones that emerge. One of today's challenges may well be expanding our capacity to think, and doing so requires returning to classic, age-old lessons: turning back to reflection, observation, and, when necessary, personal experience, using the best of the scientific method.
