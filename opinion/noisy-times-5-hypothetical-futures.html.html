Noisy Times 5: Hypothetical Futures

By Lesandro Ponciano , on November 7, 2026.

This is the final essay in the "Noisy Times" reflection series that I wrote here on Academizando throughout this year. This series was preceded by the essay "Self-Defense Against the Noises of the World" and followed by four reflections: "Noisy Times 1: The Noise of Trends", "Noisy Times 2: The Fragmentation of Life", "Noisy Times 3: The Decline of the Will to Know", and "Noisy Times 4: The Seductive Illusion". In these reflections, I tried to bring together my experience, but above all my observation of various current social phenomena. The essays are, above all, an exercise in personal reflection that sometimes identifies sources of noise in contemporary life, and at other times seeks to find logic in what is noisy.

These essays have been opinionated yet analytical regarding situations and possibilities, without the pretense of offering solutions or making predictions. One of the greatest challenges in crafting future analyses is the imponderable element that could not be considered beforehand. Anyone who made a prediction about society in 2019 may have been profoundly wrong by failing to foresee the 2020 pandemic and the structural transformations it triggered. There are other variables equally difficult to predict, such as wars and natural disasters. There are also disruptive innovations that reshape entire society, as occurred with the emergence of computers, the internet, and now advancements in artificial intelligence. Therefore, an analysis that proposes to think about future scenarios must be constantly reviewed to assess whether its initial premises remain valid or if new elements have emerged that weaken it.

There is no way to predict the future when there is so much noise. Noisy times are characterized by inconclusive transitions of social norms. In them, various anomalies are observed in relation to the previous pattern, while the new social configuration remains uncertain. It is precisely these characteristics of uncertainty and instability that make any exact prediction about the course of society impossible. However, it is possible to speculate about scenarios and possibilities. Even if they do not fully materialize, they function as baselines to guide thinking throughout evolution. Scenario thinking adopts an analytical yet speculative perspective; and this is the goal of this final essay. I see three hypotheses for the evolution of social phenomena described in Noisy Times over the coming years and decades: the hypothesis of decline, the hypothesis of elitization, and the hypothesis of progress.

The hypothesis of decline considers that society will enter a sequence of events beyond its control. Decline, in this context, must be understood at times as a broad regression, rather than extinction. It involves the shift toward a society composed of people with increasingly fragmented lives, taken by superficial and transient experiences, yielding to seductive illusions and incapable of learning significantly from their own experience. Creativity becomes extremely reduced, lacking the strength to generate profound changes. Humanity loses the capacity to understand what it has produced over the centuries, impoverishing language and multiplying isolation or merely transactional relationships. Social conflicts become constant and seemingly endless. Each attempt at change turns into just another bet that culminates in the continuity of decline. The most disturbing aspect of this hypothesis is that, should it fully materialize, no one would be able to perceive that it occurred. Memories and records of the past would have been lost; and, with the very capacity to observe and think having been compromised, the word "decline" will cease to make any sense.

The hypothesis of elitization considers that there will be stabilization, but restricted to a few. A very few people will remain sober, while the majority will be plunged into illusion. People who manage to shelter themselves from the world's noises form an "elite" not due to initial financial privilege, but because they retain skills that others have lost, such as observing, thinking, and deciding for themselves. The fact is that realities so complex and profound begin to be produced that very few people will have the capacity to comprehend them.

This elitist dynamic is not far from what has been observed over millennia, when formal and written knowledge was restricted to a small portion of the population. Universities were always few and access to them restricted. Illiteracy was the rule, and universal access to formal education is a phenomenon of less than two centuries in most countries. Historically, the elitization of knowledge and thinking is more of a norm than an exception. What this hypothesis considers is that contemporary elitization will be the portrait of an even deeper and more diverse inequality. It is not just about purchasing power or social status, but about the very capacity to observe, think, and create. Perhaps the closest analogy is with historical periods in which much of the population lived under the dominion or decisions of a few considered "enlightened".

The hypothesis of progress considers that society will contain the noise and evolve from it. Society will be able to preserve the good elements of current reality and extinguish harmful ones. Betting on this hypothesis is trusting in human capacity to analyze experiences and extract the best from them. This is reflected, for example, in recent reactions against technological excesses: the limitation of tablet use by children, the ban on cell phones in schools, and restrictions on social media use by minors. Although it took time, society has begun reacting to technologies that generated addiction, social destabilization, and cognitive decline. Believing in progress means considering that artificial intelligences will not cancel out human thinking capacity and that humans will be able to regulate and limit the use of these tools. It is to assess that society retains sufficient rationality not to submerge into the seductive illusion.

Part of the noises of the contemporary world comes from the dispute among these three possibilities: decline, elitization, and progress. First, there are those who believe that human beings always adapt to new realities and progress from them (the progress hypothesis). In this camp are, for example, accelerationists: a philosophical, political, and cultural movement that advocates drastically accelerating and expanding technology and capitalism to provoke profound transformations. Second, there are those who bet that new technologies will take the place of humans (the decline hypothesis), believing that humanity will lose control. In the case of artificial intelligence, they warn that society may be marginalized or exterminated by it; these are often called doomerists. Finally, there are those who view society as an inevitably unequal structure, in which some benefit and subjugate others (the elitization hypothesis). This is the case with analyses regarding digital authoritarianism or technological totalitarianism. Proponents of this reading believe that the race for artificial general intelligence will create an insurmountable competitive advantage, where the company or country that achieves it first will subjugate all others, consolidating the logic that "the winner takes all".

Depending on which of these views a person adopts, their stance toward current dilemmas changes radically. There are those who see a brave new world, those who fear an apocalypse, and those who believe nothing will change substantially. Contemporary society is marked by dilemmas that humans find difficult to equilibrate. Several problems manifest simultaneously: wars, climate change, economic crises, social dissatisfactions, and technological dysfunctions. Each of them brings the same doubt: mitigate the situation through reforms that avoid disaster, or stay the course and accelerate the pace trusting in the capacity to adapt to risks? Furthermore, the three central hypotheses can decompose into sub-hipotheses, materializing only in part. The hypothesis that becomes real may vary for each context.

Another analytical perspective is to view these scenarios as evolutionary stages, rather than mutually exclusive alternatives. The combination of stages can be diverse. First, there is a belief in the capacity for adaptation to advance toward something better (the progress hypothesis). Next, it is realized that this progress does not reach everyone; benefits are captured by a smaller group of people, such as the younger, more creative, educated, or those with greater purchasing power (the elitization hypothesis). Finally, this very group is seduced by the power it has obtained and loses control of the system that imagined it dominated (the decline hypothesis). In this staged perspective, one innovates by understanding decline as an intermediary stage, rather than a definitive one. This would open room for a reversal of decline. This is what seems to occur, for example, with current attempts to reverse the excessive use of screens and social media. Such a reversal may be possible when degradation affects only a portion of society, or when exposure to noisy technologies has not compromised the cognitive capacity of all individuals.

This series of essays on Noisy Times emphasized the situation of decline in education, daily life, online behavior, and the uncontrolled use of technologies. This focus served to give perspective to the risks, but it does not mean I consider the decline scenario as the most likely or that we are facing a generalized cognitive collapse. Nor do I consider the progress scenario the closest. What I see on the horizon is a restructuring of society into an elitization. On the one hand, many people barely understand what happens around them while jumping from trend to trend on social media, consuming contents generated by artificial intelligence without knowing what they are, or entering universities seeking only a diploma rather than the training of skills and knowledge. On the other hand, there are the creators of these trends who reap popularity benefits, the owners of platforms who profit from engagement, the artificial intelligence developers who preserve spaces of influence, and those who take advantage of others' ignorance and naivety regarding technology. There is, therefore, a group that concentrates power and protects itself from the noise precisely because it produces and controls it. Whether this ongoing elitization will culminate in progress or decline is something that remains beyond our visual reach; it is a future that is contested and will be determined by upcoming social, economic, and political struggles.
