Noisy Times 2: The Fragmentation of Life

By Lesandro Ponciano , on August 27, 2026.

In 2013, during my PhD studies, I noticed a peculiar behavior: volunteers who did not make lasting contributions. They were the so-called transients, individuals who contributed just once to online platforms and never returned. We initially thought this was a phenomenon unique to digital spaces, but years later I came across a study reporting the exact same pattern in in-person volunteering. Long-term commitment had been replaced by the pursuit of temporary, bite-sized experiences. This dynamic soon became visible in the job market, with young professionals refusing to stay at a company for more than a few months under the premise of seeking a wider diversity of "experiences." That was how my interest in this fragmentation of life began.

This rush for instant gratification and for the part at the expense of the whole has also breached classroom walls. Around 2019, while teaching evaluation methods in my Human-Computer Interaction (HCI) course, a student made an unusual request: he wanted a certificate for every single method taught, such as Heuristic Evaluation and Usability Testing. I covered over 10 methods throughout the term. To this student, earning the degree of bachelor or engineer felt insufficient, and waiting four years to receive it felt like an eternity; he craved a series of periodic micro-certifications along the way. Today, this logic is glaringly obvious on platforms like LinkedIn, where finishing a two-hour online course often draws far more applause than a degree built over years of effort.

The digital environment is undeniably the primary catalyst of this phenomenon. Platforms like Instagram, TikTok, and Twitter (X) operate on a continuous feedback loop that prioritizes short, hyper-stimulating content. As discussed in the previous post, The Noise of Trends, algorithms incentivize this behavior. People become conditioned to the convenience of fast, superficial content and lose the capacity to absorb longer, more demanding material. Online social media has turned any reflection longer than two paragraphs into the derogatory label "textão" (a walls-of-text post). Everything must be wrapped in sensational emotional appeal (clickbait), be shallow enough to hook instant attention, and ideally ride the coattails of a trending topic. This wouldn't be a major issue if it were confined strictly to entertainment. But it is no longer confined. People are confusing education with digital entertainment, and pedagogy with mass communication.

The result is a swift, silent erosion of our capacity for deep thought. We live in an era of fragmented lives, where many content creators—so-called influencers—lack the depth to offer anything beneath the surface, while audiences lose the cognitive stamina required to digest dense material. The importance of immersive reading is gradually being forgotten. It is common to see posts on LinkedIn and Instagram attempting to explain complex subjects—such as Gestalt Principles or Cognitive Engineering—by completely stripping away their foundations, contexts, and underlying assumptions. The reader consumes that decontextualized pill of information and acquires the dangerous illusion of having mastered the subject.

This same superficiality leaks into personal life experiences. There is a popular modern narrative that younger generations seek to collect diverse experiences and "build memories," yet actual practice often contradicts the claim. Consider those who curate Instagram carousels showcasing visits to half a dozen European countries, having spent under 24 hours in each. The visual receipt exists, but the lived experience is absent. What remains is merely an external memory. The trip was so rushed that while the body passed through the location, the location left no footprint on the person. What is the true value of this kind of experience? Much like the relentless scrolling of short-form videos, these experiences fail to connect, settle, or contribute to meaningful personal growth. A trip becomes nothing more than a photo in a feed.

Often, this fragmentation is not even a conscious choice; it is a symptom of collective exhaustion. Daily routines are so fractured by competing tasks that human memory itself has become a scarce resource. I recall meeting up with a colleague in 2024 to resume a project we had co-developed back in 2020. To my surprise, she began explaining the project to me from scratch, convinced she had built it entirely on her own. I had to pull up our old emails and draft versions to remind her of my co-authorship—and to show her she didn't need to spend hours explaining it to me. She had simply lost that memory amidst the daily avalanche of information she was forced to manage. She was only convinced when confronted with the historical records she had forgotten, but which left no room for doubt.

To manage these noises of the world, we are becoming increasingly dependent on external logs. Digital records have become our sole mechanism for proving to ourselves and others what we have actually lived. Yet if we can no longer recall our own lived experiences unaided, what is the ultimate purpose of those experiences? Remembering is only the foundational layer of learning—a process that must progress through examining, associating, comparing, evaluating, and appreciating, eventually reaching higher levels such as synthesizing new insights from past events.

This year brought the Football World Cup, a sport deeply cherished across all social classes in Brazil, and the country lost once again. The behavior of some players stood out: it seemed they were not aiming to win the match or even claim the championship title, but merely seeking that single highlight—those few seconds of a dribble or a goal to be clipped and go viral on platforms like TikTok, as if the ultimate defeat did not matter. The need for a beginning, a middle, and a targed end has been lost. In music, it is common to observe an introduction, a chorus (the climax that sometimes repeats itself), and an ending. I saw a trending TikTok video featuring a background track of less than 15 seconds. When I searched for the original song from which that snippet had been extracted, I discovered it didn't exist. The creator had produced only the fragment—just what would have been the climax, the chorus. It is as if the introduction and the ending no longer mattered. This is striking in a popular culture that once produced works like "Faroeste Caboclo," written by Renato Russo, spanning 9 minutes and 3 seconds of a fascinating story. Today, it seems all that matters are the moments of climax, regardless of the final outcome.

In the poem "Cidadezinha qualquer" (Any Little Town), published in 1930, Brazilian poet Carlos Drummond de Andrade described the slow monotony of small-town life: “Casas entre bananeiras / mulheres entre laranjeiras / pomar amor cantar. / Um homem vai devagar. / Um cachorro vai devagar. / Um burro vai devagar. / Devagar... as janelas olham. / Eta vida besta, meu Deus.” (Houses among banana trees / women among orange trees / orchard love singing. / A man walks slowly. / A dog walks slowly. / A donkey walks slowly. / Slowly... the windows watch. / What a silly life, my God.) Today, that contemplative monotony has evaporated. Smartphones, accessible even in the smallest villages, instantly teleport individuals into another dimension—one where they drown in a flood of rapid, intense, and trivial content: stories blending reality, creative fantasies, and AI-generated illusions. A few screen taps on messaging apps give the illusion of instantaneous connection with the entire globe.

In contemporary society, there is no longer room for a slow, contemplative gaze upon a single landscape. Nor is there a life dedicated to a single pursuit. It is no longer clear whether people possess anything they can truly call a "life project." Consequently, the debate shifts from the adjective used to describe life to the noun itself. The core question is no longer whether life is "silly," as Drummond observed, but whether the disjointed fragments we collect actually constitute a "life" or something else entirely.

I do not dismiss the argument that we live in fragments because we inhabit an era of continuous disruption driven by technological, economic, social, political, and climate crises. In a world in perpetual crisis and rapid transformation, nothing feels permanent. Likewise, devoting oneself permanently to something that might disappear tomorrow can seem unappealing. Excessive fragmentation and detachment can thus serve as a form of self-preservation. Yet, if that is the diagnosis, we must still ask: what grants coherence to a human being? Isn't it necessary to gather the fragments to build a whole self? What will become of us if we lose the ability to remember what we have lived?

In academia, fragmentation manifests as information overload and an unyielding pursuit of quantity over quality. Collecting superficial achievements creates the illusion of extensive experience and deep knowledge. Some researchers accumulate hundreds of papers across conferences and journals, publishing dozens of articles per year. Yet many cannot recall the title of a specific paper, let alone its methodology or findings. Without genuine immersion, scientific inquiry becomes hollow and a research paper degenerates into a mere line on a resume. Life, sliced into disconnected snippets, loses the depth required to mature into true wisdom. In these noisy times, as life fragments, we flirt with the tragic possibility of losing our capacity to think deeply about it.
