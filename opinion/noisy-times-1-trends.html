Noisy Times 1: The Noise of Trends

By Lesandro Ponciano , on August 7, 2026.

Online, certain topics suddenly become trends. All of a sudden, it seems everyone is talking about the exact same thing; shortly after, the topic vanishes as if it never existed. Subjects come in and go out of style in a flash. But how does this affect the behavior of those who produce and consume this type of content? The goal of this post is to analyze this question through the following discussions:

  • How do trends emerge?
  • How do trends relate to the attention economy?
  • Can trend dynamics warp our perception of importance?
  • Actively seeking relevant information vs. passively receiving irrelevant information: what should we do?
  • Is whatever new technology brings always better than the old?
  • Trends in other domains: Academia

How do trends emerge?

In a natural trend, a vast number of people discuss a topic like "tsunami" when a real-world disaster strikes. On the other hand, a trend is artificial when, with no clear external trigger, that same mass of people suddenly starts talking about "tanghulu" or a viral dessert craze.

A natural trend stems from events outside the media system distributing the information. An artificial trend, however, is usually generated within the system itself: someone posts a video about a viral food item that captures audience attention, and other creators—looking for content ideas—realize they can piggyback on the hype to get engagement. Or someone creates a choreography for a song clip, the video garners millions of views, and other creators hurry to recreate it. Platforms like TikTok, which make it effortless to remix content using shared audio, greatly accelerate this phenomenon.

Trends can also be heavily pushed by content distribution algorithms across social networks and digital publishing platforms. In computer science, algorithms based on popularity and recency are widely adopted due to their high practical efficiency, drawing on principles like temporal locality. Thus, when a system detects a sudden spike of interest around a topic, it promotes it further, assuming it represents the crowd's current focal point. If a few users start tweeting about an earthquake, the algorithm assumes it is a breaking topic on the verge of going viral and begins circulating more posts about it.

How do trends relate to the attention economy?

Relying on algorithms that weigh recency and popularity can be quite effective in specific situations: a national holiday (everyone talking about Independence Day on that date), a major sporting event (everyone commenting on a championship finale), or a natural disaster (everyone monitoring a hurricane). However, the collateral damage is clear.

Content creators—who depend on online visibility for their livelihood—have a direct financial incentive to "ride the trend" to maximize ad revenue. Covering the topic of the moment yields higher view counts and, consequently, bigger payouts. This fuels artificial trends: people write, record, or produce videos on a topic not because they have anything meaningful to add, but simply because that is what the algorithm is rewarding at that moment.

Even creators who choose not to jump on the bandwagon cannot escape its influence. The mechanics of algorithmic feeds dictate the rules of the attention economy. For instance, last month I came across a community note on a YouTube creator's channel that read something like this:

"We usually upload a new video every Monday. But this Monday, the national soccer team plays in the World Cup, and all of YouTube will be talking about nothing else. Therefore, we won't publish today because the algorithm will suppress our video for not being about the dominant topic. We will be back next week."

Can trend dynamics warp our perception of importance?

When dealing with lighthearted entertainment trends—such as a viral dance challenge—the societal risk is minimal. The problem arises when trend mechanics hijack serious issues, such as the adultification of children and teenagers. A vital, complex issue cannot simply be tossed aside when the hype dies down just because it no longer generates clicks or revenue.

It is easy to think of critical issues that never turn into trending topics—especially complex, long-term challenges like persistent educational deficits or climate change projections. Similarly, when a critical issue briefly becomes a trend, its fading leaves a false impression that the problem has been solved or lost its gravity—such as political corruption scandals or the plight of vulnerable unhoused populations suffering from substance dependence. Just because something stopped trending does not mean it ceased to exist. Likewise, if something never trended, that does not mean it was never important.

I saw part of this debate surface recently in a video about minimalism. Minimalism had a massive spike in popularity (a trend) years ago, and over time, search interest cooled off; the video questioned whether the concept itself had lost its relevance. Expanding on this point, it is crucial to analyze the relationship between "relevance" and "trending status" on the web, and how the disconnect between the two degrades our online experience. Viewing discussions strictly through the lens of trends inevitably flattens and trivializes complex debates.

Minimalism, for example, encompasses multiple dimensions. When viewed strictly as a lifestyle, it is often criticized as an elitist privilege—tailored to affluent individuals who choose to consume less, which feels completely disconnected from the reality of countries like Brazil, where a vast portion of the population lacks access to basic necessities. On the other hand, my primary engagement with the concept comes from Design and Human-Computer Interaction (HCI). In digital interface design, minimalism is applied to improve user efficiency and satisfaction by promoting simplicity and minimizing cognitive load on short-term memory. The more irrelevant clutter fills a screen, the harder a user has to work to find what they need, leading to longer execution times and higher error rates. This is a functional, positive form of minimalism. Therefore, reducing minimalism to a trendy lifestyle—or declaring it obsolete because its social media iteration proved problematic—is a fundamental misunderstanding.

Actively seeking relevant information vs. passively receiving irrelevant information: what should we do?

The logic of trends and its accompanying flaws are relatively recent phenomena, driven by the rise of recommendation algorithms and infinite scrolling over the last two decades. While reading the comments under a video, I ran into an insightful reflection written by an anonymous user:

"Something I pulled back up after a long time away was an RSS feed. It's hard to express how good it feels to have pull-down media rather than push media, just being able to choose 'oh, I want news stories from these sites, videos from these people, podcast episodes from this list', all curated intentionally without the opportunity for random junk to be pushed alongside."

The dominant architecture of modern platforms operates by forcefully pushing content onto users (push), rather than empowering them to select what they want to consume (pull). A trend is precisely an effort to force-feed information under the assumption that it is relevant simply because it is popular at that split second. Content creators seeking reach align themselves with trends to be favored by algorithms; meanwhile, many users passively accept the stream. Actively searching for quality information demands far more effort than passively consuming whatever hits the screen. However, this becomes deeply problematic when individuals lose sight of how their minds are being shaped by content they never explicitly chose to view.

Users who recognize this passive manipulation often search for alternatives. Some platforms offer settings to reduce recommendation feeds—YouTube, for instance, allows users to pause watch history to clean up home page suggestions. However, due to the cognitive bias known as the default effect, the vast majority of people leave default settings untouched, as changing them requires deliberate effort. The growing movement back toward vintage web tools by some users reflects more than nostalgia: it demonstrates a realization that older media consumption models were functionally superior.

Is whatever new technology brings always better than the old?

In the digital realm, a phenomenon long seen in physical products is becoming increasingly apparent: the loss of lasting value. We purchase state-of-the-art smartphones engineered with planned obsolescence and short lifespans, whereas older basic mobile phones (feature phones), despite their limited features, offered extraordinary durability. Similar critiques are made of modern home appliances. Technological iteration in specific domains does not automatically translate to globally superior products.

Criticizing present-day digital ecosystems by comparing them to past solutions is often unfairly dismissed as mere nostalgia. In 2025, I challenged a class of Human-Computer Interaction students to analyze discontinued platforms and contrast them with emerging digital services. The students produced a documentary titled "A Internet Está Chata?" (Is the Internet Boring?), whose core themes I expanded upon in the article Memórias digitais e presente conectado: a Internet está chata?. Although this field requires ongoing scientific study, there is a clear decline in the diversity of online experiences. Trends have become the default mechanism for creators attempting to gain traction in saturated global markets. The end result is content homogenization and the erosion of the rich, diverse online ecosystem we enjoyed in the early days of the web.

This dynamic reaches an even more critical turning point with the mass adoption of generative AI in content creation. In pursuit of higher margins and scale, many creators now outsource writing, scripting, and artwork to automated models optimized to feed algorithmic demands. This drives an explosive growth in artificial trends and an unprecedented standardization of digital media. Far from improving the quality of our online lives, reckless automation fosters a hyper-produced yet generic web flooded with noise. In this environment, genuine relevance is drowned out by a deluge of content crafted solely to manipulate metrics.

Trends in other domains: Academia

The clearest parallel to this dynamic outside the consumer web can be found within academia itself. Regrettably, it has become increasingly common for researchers to anchor their entire careers around trending buzzwords.

Some scientists select their research directions based on whatever term is currently hot: if LLMs (Large Language Models) are trending, they pivot to LLMs; if Cloud Computing gains momentum, they shift to Cloud; the same occurs with SRE (Site Reliability Engineering), IoT (Internet of Things), Social Networks, Ubiquitous Systems, Pervasive Systems, and so on.

Computer Science constantly cycles from one fad to another. As Donald Knuth famously warned—a point I previously highlighted in an article on defining scientific research topics—"Do not believe that simply because something is trending it is good." Scholars focused purely on raw publication counts or citation metrics tend to follow these trends. Their motivations mirror those on social media: riding the wave of popular topics to sustain high output volumes and maintain prominence in grant funding and evaluation metrics.

The consequences are damaging: foundational, unresolved problems are abandoned simply because they lost their immediate allure. These lingering challenges often require greater effort, yield less instant publicity, and are harder to publish, yet their real-world impact is incomparably higher.

Analyzing the publication records of certain researchers reveals a patchwork of papers on fragmented, trendy topics without the foundation of a cohesive body of work or a lasting contribution to human knowledge. This loses sight of the primary goal of science—advancing our understanding of phenomena—and fuels the flawed belief that doing science is merely about mass-producing papers.

Ultimately, the noise of trends is not merely an internet phenomenon, but a structural reflection of how the attention economy has reshaped our relationship with information, system design, and even scientific progress. By prioritizing the ephemeral and the viral, we risk homogenizing our digital experiences and neglecting complex, long-standing, and vital problems. Regaining control over what we consume, restoring depth to our discourse, and resisting the urge to let algorithms dictate our choices is more than a technical preference: it is an essential act of self-defense against the noise of our era.
