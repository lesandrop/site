Noisy Times 4: The Seductive Illusion

By Lesandro Ponciano , on October 3, 2026.

What happens in a society where people live from trend to trend, whose lives are organized around shallow and erratic experiences, and who want to think less and less? One of the consequences of this may be the corrosion of the ability to build a mental repertoire to analyze this reality. Assuming that this corrosion is one of the possibilities, what emerges from it? Judging by historical experience, whenever the ability to know and think is corroded, illusion emerges. A distorted perception arises, an erroneous interpretation of reality: a constant deception of the senses and the mind. We flirt with the possibility of not being able to discern whether we are progressing towards a better understanding of phenomena or if, as already experienced in the past, we are regressing or inaugurating ages of darkness.

For most people, it is very conveniently pleasant for artificial intelligences (AIs) to know and think for them. Often a decision needs to be made, but articulating information in search of the best alternative takes work, time, and effort. AI makes it possible for a person not to have to pore over a large amount of information to make a decision based on it. After all, AI holds the data and can articulate it to analyze the space of possible decisions. Human beings then begin to base their actions on recommendations from a machine, trusting an analysis process they do not understand and information they have not analyzed themselves. The person merely "believes" and accepts. Cognitive biases favor this phenomenon: human beings tend to overestimate how much they know or understand about certain subjects. Therefore, they imagine they know what the AI does and how it arrived at that suggestion. A layperson with a smartphone in their hands believes they are a PhD.

The concept of credibility is generally associated with "how much one can believe" in a given piece of data, in the channel through which the data was received, or in the emitter of that data. There are several ways to evaluate credibility. Before Aristotle and the creation of logic as a form of deep reasoning that allows evaluating information, people relied heavily on the eloquence of the emitter's emotional rhetoric. In that view, the better a communicator was, the more beautifully they spoke and "moved" the audience, the more credibility people gave them; the criterion was not the verification or analysis of arguments. It is not absurd to think that when the ability to know and think is reduced, we return to this type of misguided evaluation. Therefore, it is not surprising to see a YouTuber or TikToker who builds flawed reasoning but is followed by many. The same can be said about the analysis people make of what is said by certain coaches, politicians, and religious leaders. A similar phenomenon occurs when reading an AI hallucination without the ability to understand that it is an error. It is an enchanting and deceitful discourse.

"Sincere lies interest me," say Cazuza and Maurício Einhorn in a famous verse of the song Maior Abandonado. Even when one is capable of analyzing reality, realizing that it is harsh makes the illusion seductive. Action based on illusion can be very attractive. The effort it requires is incomparably smaller than what is necessary to act rationally, based on the process of knowing and thinking. Besides being attractive, it becomes seductive when it offers something that seems more pleasant than reason itself. As David Le Breton shows in the book Desaparecer de Si: Uma Tentação Contemporânea, when the capacity to deal with a hostile reality is not developed, escaping (even from ourselves) becomes a constant. This evasion is common in phenomena such as addiction to games, drugs, social aversions, and various other forms of isolation.

Illusion is also convenient for opportunism. In the practice of teaching, it was very common for students to ask about the learning assessment in the days leading up to it. I would say that everyone should study to get good results. Some, however, would respond in an ironic tone: "getting good results only depends on you, professor". Behind this phrase there are two elements. The first is that the professor determines how the assessment will be; therefore, if they want the students to have good results, it is enough to create an assessment based on trivialities. The second element is that the person who grades and assigns the score is also the professor, depending solely on them to assign a good grade to students, regardless of whether they demonstrated learning. It is not rare for students to believe this, thinking that learning assessment is a game of convenience.

I asked the following questions to several veteran professors: "Is the learning assessment you apply today the same, harder, or easier than the one you applied 10 years ago? Is your strictness in grading today the same, greater, or lesser?". All of them made their assessments easier and became less strict in grading. One of them even said: "if I were to apply the assessment from 10 years ago today, no student would pass". Another reported: "there is a subject I always included in the assessment, but since most students got it wrong, I stopped including it". Some might say that perhaps they were too demanding and adapted along the way. However, the reality is that no professor teaches complex content without paying a high price, even if the demand is at the appropriate level. Over time, many stop "swimming against the tide". The lower the students' ability to deal with the complexity of the content, the more the professor tends to adopt this behavior. At the extreme, we arrive at the illusion: the professor pretends to teach, the students pretend to learn, and everyone is "happy" (I would say, "deluded").

Nowadays, it is not uncommon in Brazilian universities to encounter the idea that students must be happy and satisfied. I do not disagree with this, but it is important to remember that a university is not an entertainment club; or, at least, that is not how it is legally and socially instituted. In some universities, joy and satisfaction are monitored through processes in which students grade professors. When these grades show that the class is not "happy and satisfied", the professor suffers sanctions. The problem with this approach is presuming that the learning process is always "happy" and that it invariably results in "satisfaction". This is an illusion. Many students do not achieve the expected results, fail, and no one is happy and satisfied with a failing grade. Currently, the human parameter for satisfaction is ordering food via delivery and receiving it instantly, or spending hours watching short videos that arouse quick emotions and require no effort at all.

Learning is not like ordering a delivery and sitting around waiting for the food to arrive without delays. Learning is more like cultivating the ingredients, caring for them so they grow, and preparing the meal. It is a long, laborious process full of uncertainties. To master a certain subject, it is often necessary to read the material repeatedly and solve dozens of exercises. It is necessary to sit for hours, giving up watching a series or talking with friends, and even so, the desired result may not be achieved. In complex areas such as engineering, mathematics, physics, and computer science, learning is an arduous process. Not because it is made to be arduous, but because the topics studied are complex, having been produced over centuries of study by very brilliant minds. If the student is not prepared for this type of content, one cannot expect them to feel happy all the time when confronted with it. In this case, the lack of "happiness and satisfaction" says more about the student than about the professor. Attributing this responsibility to the professor pushes them toward the opportunistic illusion. This is what leads, for example, a Calculus 2 professor to think their job is to take a pleasant and happy stroll with the students around the campus square instead of teaching the dreadful integrals.

Over the last few years, society has built a digital world heavily based on the aesthetics of colors, sounds, and intonations. An emotional semiotics driven by likes, reactions, and view counts was adopted. These are fragile engagement metrics; at most, they allow measuring the initial attention a piece of content receives. When it comes to videos, the number of views says almost nothing. In a video with 10,000 views and a 10-minute duration, for example, YouTube considers it a good result if 50% of the people make it past the first 30 seconds. In other words, it would not be shocking to discover that absolutely no one watched the video in its entirety, even though it displays thousands of views. For entertainment, perhaps this is good enough, but for learning, it certainly is not.

I was once in a meeting about online classes where a leading figure in online education was speaking. He recounted a conflict between a traditional professor and his student. The student had recorded a video to teach one of the course's topics online. However, the material contained factual slips and inaccurate information. Even so, it had already accumulated over 50,000 views on a platform. When alerted by the professor about the errors, the student replied that it wasn't a problem. She compared the factual deviations in her video to a Brazilian funk song in which the author inserts vulgar words or grammatical errors just to make it catchy. Surprisingly, the speaker concluded that the student was right. For him, the professor was just jealous, as the video had already reached a larger audience than the number of students the professor had taught in his entire life. The comparison between "views" and the professor's students was enough for me to get up and leave. I felt as though I were giving a fair hearing to a view I considered mistaken. Learning is not entertainment. But the capacity of many people to think critically about this is, indeed, limited.

When carefully analyzing current society and how it works, it is not difficult to understand the degradation of important indicators, such as the capacity to maintain attention in a lasting way, mathematical reasoning (PISA), and even IQ. I am not saying these indicators are perfect, nor comparing one indicator with another, but highlighting that the same indicator shows a deterioration over time. It is also not surprising that content of low informational quality or little challenge becomes so popular. The concept of "brainrot" has become popular in recent years, but it does not fully describe the problem. The real danger is what a society formed by people with brainrot turns into. From this perspective, the rise of religion and pseudosciences in situations where reason should prevail is not surprising. The reality can prove to be so challenging that it surpasses Carl Sagan's analysis in The Demon-Haunted World. Science as a Candle in the Dark. The deterioration is not something foreseen for the metaverse or a potential virtual reality; it already shows itself as real in the online life implemented by conventional systems. It is no coincidence that the digital space has become fertile ground for extremist ideologies and miraculous explanations. The digital world today is a space that appeals to emotions, not a place where reason prevails.

But what develops in the digital environment is not restricted to it; it overflows into the entire society. Among the various themes that spread through the networks, in 2025 we had the case of reborn babies. They were ultra-realistic dolls in size, texture, and shape, making it very difficult to distinguish them from a real child. Many influencers started showing their lives caring for these dolls as if they were real children. Naturally, there were those who saw this as a behavior of extreme futility, but the reaction was not limited to comments on the internet. In June 2025, in an upscale neighborhood of Belo Horizonte, a man slapped the face of a real 4-month-old baby, thinking it was a doll. The child suffered injuries and had to be taken to the hospital. The aggressor was restrained, detained, and punished with a fine. This is one of the pinnacles of illusion and leaves us with two serious warnings. The first is the inability to discern what is real in the physical environment based on behaviors shaped in the digital one. The second is the trivialization of relationships to the point of attacking another's possession (which would already be inappropriate in the case of a doll, and is a criminal absurdity in the case of a baby).

There are two interrelated risks that can greatly worsen this scenario of seductive illusion. The first is building a bizarre world and treating it as natural simply because we do not remember what life was like before this new reality. Those who have no memory of what the world was like before the noise (or who were born immersed in it) might not even know that it is possible to live without this noise. What started as an anomalous point has become the new norm. Ignorance, illusion, and the inability to think can lead to the destruction of something good to put something much worse in its place. And worst of all: without us having the cognitive capacity to realize this is happening. It is similar to our current difficulty in understanding how great works in architecture and mathematics were achieved by ancient civilizations. We well know that history is not linear nor does it follow a continuous process of progress; ages of darkness and decline often emerge. It is precisely our capacity to think and avoid darkness or decline that has been deteriorated by our newest technological and social "innovations".
